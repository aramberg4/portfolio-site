We gave seven algorithms $10,000 each. A month later, one has made basically all the money.

A day-33 retrospective: the reformed leaderboard-copier got hot, the clever filters merely survived, the copy-everything control quietly lost — and six of seven books have re-bet the Fed.

Thirty-three days ago, Polywatch started running seven paper-trading algorithms against live Polymarket data — each seeded with $10,000, each expressing a different theory about whose trades are worth copying (or fading). Two of them have already earned their own post-mortems: Top 10 Mirror lost 9% in eleven days copying arbitrage bots, and Inverse Losers went first-to-worst in a single Fed announcement. This is the wider-angle review: every algo, what it's good at, where it's broken, and what a month of live data actually supports.

60-second explainer — the scoreboard has three clocks

Not every algo has been running the same race. Four started July 16. Insider Echo and Sharp Follow were reset on July 22, when we lowered the ingestion floor to $500 for classified wallets — their original incarnations had gone almost signal-less at the $10K whale threshold. Top 10 Mirror was torn down and rebuilt on July 28 after its first version turned out to be copying arb bots. So the board mixes 32-day, 27-day, and 20-day track records, and the shortest one has the biggest number on it.

Reading the table: profit factor is gross wins ÷ gross losses on settled positions — above 1.0 means the wins actually outweigh the losses; win rate alone can't tell you that, as Everyman is about to demonstrate.

Top 10 Mirror: +38%, deserved asterisks, and a heart attack

The algo we publicly executed three weeks ago is now carrying the entire experiment. The v3 rebuild — rank wallets by lifetime PnL, require 20+ trades in the last 30 days, reject anything that trades like a bot — produced a roster of genuine sports bettors, and the mirror became a high-velocity sports book: MLB, UFC, League of Legends, Dota 2, tennis. Median hold time is 2.4 hours. It has settled 325 positions in 20 days — more than the other six algos combined — at a 56% win rate and a 1.34 profit factor. That's the healthiest evidence on the board: a modest edge, huge sample, actually realized.

It is also the least relaxing thing we've ever charted:

The night of August 15–16 is the strategy in miniature. It went into the evening at an all-time high of $14,717, then a UFC 330 card and an MLS slate went against the roster: −$938 on Turner–Fernandes, −$609 on Makhachev–Garry, −$459 on Dern–Robertson, and −$1,323 on a single CF Montréal match — that last one a full 10%-of-equity position, the maximum the sizing rules allow. By 08:42 UTC equity was $10,681, a 27.4% drawdown in under eight hours. Then the next day's slate won it most of the way back. Realized PnL for that whole bloodbath of an August 16? Plus $128.

Strengths: the only algo with a large settled sample; velocity means marks become cash daily instead of festering; the bot gate demonstrably fixed v2's core failure (v2 recorded zero mirror exits ever — v3 has already mirror-exited nine times for +$1,412). It also holds the experiment's only real diversification: zero macro exposure, purely because its bettors live in sports.

Weaknesses: the volatility is structural, not bad luck — max-cap positions on single matches, correlated slates settling within hours of each other, and a roster that rotates every six hours means the book can be one bad fight card from flat. A 27% drawdown would shake real money out of most humans. And 20 days is still 20 days; its own v2 looked defensible for eleven.

VerdictThe bet that "genuine winners exist and are identifiable by lifetime PnL plus a bot filter" is the best-supported claim in the experiment — and the risk profile is the price of admission. Watch the drawdowns, not the return.

Kitchen Sink: the quiet winner nobody's talking about

Kitchen Sink scores every signal — insider +3, smart money +2, decent whale +1, size bonus, extra-wallet bonus — and only enters at conviction ≥ 4. The result is the best risk-adjusted line on the board: +9.3% with a 5.6% max drawdown and a 1.87 profit factor, the highest of the seven. It trades selectively (98 entries in 32 days), takes profit at +60%, stops at −50%, and — the clever part — latches when a position marks 90¢ or better and rides it to resolution instead of selling early.

The latch is quietly the best micro-decision in the codebase: latched positions have gone 25-for-27, banking +$507, versus a slightly negative record on unlatched holds. The exit stack overall: take-profits +$817, latched rides +$507, stop-losses −$436. Every exit rule is pulling its weight.

Strengths: the conviction gate turns the same signal stream that loses money raw (see Everyman, below) into a profitable book; drawdown discipline; exits that lock gains instead of donating them back.

Weaknesses: 55 settled positions is a small sample for a nine-point-return claim. And its open book herds with every other classified algo — 78% of its deployed cost sits in three macro events (Strait of Hormuz, a US-invasion market, and the September Fed meeting). Its 5.6% drawdown history was earned in a month when those correlated bets mostly went fine.

VerdictThe most promising design in the experiment: score, gate, take profit, latch sure things. Needs another month and an event-concentration cap before we trust the drawdown number.

Crowd Surge: consensus is real, and worth about five points

Crowd Surge waits for two or more tracked wallets to buy the same outcome within 24 hours. It's +5.5% with tidy risk (6.7% max drawdown, 1.40 profit factor) — but the decomposition is lopsided: its 13 take-profit exits made +$1,177, while its 31 hold-to-resolution positions netted −$96 and its 21 stop-losses cost −$612. In other words, all of the profit came from selling into mark-ups; positions that had to actually be right at resolution roughly broke even.

Strengths: consensus among tracked wallets does seem to precede favorable price moves often enough to harvest; risk contained; steady.

Weaknesses: the edge looks like momentum-capture, not truth-finding — which means it depends on the +50% take-profit trigger being there to cash it. It also shares Kitchen Sink's herd problem (61% of its open cost in the same three macro events) and its top source wallets are the same handful every other classified algo follows.

VerdictKeep it, but understand what it is: a mark-up harvester on crowd momentum. If the take-profit rule ever gets removed, this algo probably stops working.

Insider Echo: right 74% of the time, paid almost nothing for it

The purest hypothesis — copy every buy from an insider-classified wallet, hold to resolution — produces the best win rate on the board (74.5%) and almost no money: +$300 realized, profit factor 1.12, meaning $2,900 of gross wins were nearly cancelled by $2,601 of gross losses. The shape is classic favorite-copying: median entry at 61¢, lots of small wins collecting the last 30–40 cents, then occasional face-rippers like −$671 on a WTI-crude July market that went to zero. Its 17.8% max drawdown — earned in its very first week post-reset — is wildly out of proportion to its 2% return.

There's also a structural problem: with no exit policy and a slow resolution calendar, it's fully deployed — $8,751 of its capital is locked in 45 open positions (many in long-dated Iran-war markets), leaving $1,549 of cash. New insider signals now get sized off whatever's left. The algo is a roach motel: signals check in, capital doesn't check out.

Strengths: the classification itself looks real — the wallets it follows are genuinely right far more often than not, and its biggest source wallet is up ~$54K lifetime. As a signal detector, it works.

Weaknesses: being right late, at 61¢, hold-to-resolution, captures a sliver of edge and all of the risk; no exits means no compounding and no defense; capital lockup starves it of exactly the signals it exists to catch.

VerdictGreat detector, bad trader. The obvious v3: keep the signal, add Kitchen Sink's exit stack, and cap hold duration so the book breathes.

Everyman: the control did its job — by losing

Everyman copies every qualifying signal with no classification filter. It sits at +1.0%, but that's marks: its realized PnL is −$371 with a profit factor of 0.71, despite a 70% win rate — many small wins buying favorites at a median 72¢, outweighed by fewer, larger losses. It spent most of the month underwater (low: $9,611) and only crossed back over $10,000 in August on unrealized gains across its sprawling 138-position book. It even holds both sides of 25 different markets simultaneously, because the crowd it copies disagrees with itself — paying the 1¢ slippage twice to guarantee at most $1 back.

This is the most useful negative result we have. Same stream, no gate: PF 0.71. Kitchen Sink's gated version of the same stream: PF 1.87. That gap — not any single algo's return — is the cleanest evidence in the experiment that the classification layer is doing something.

Strengths: exists, loses honestly, makes every other number interpretable.

Weaknesses: none that matter; it's a benchmark. Though its +1.0% scoreboard line flatters it — realized, it's the third-worst algo.

VerdictCopying whale-sized flow indiscriminately loses money after slippage. Good. That's exactly what a control should prove.

Sharp Follow: the exits work, the holds bleed

Sharp Follow copies insiders and smart money, and — uniquely among the classified algos — mirror-exits when the source wallet sells. The month split it cleanly in two: its 40 mirror exits made +$751 (its best single exit: +$584, out of a WTI position the source dumped in time), while the 53 positions it held to resolution lost −$1,194. When the sharp it's copying actually manages the trade, following works. When the sharp just… never sells, Sharp Follow becomes Insider Echo with worse entries, and pays for it.

It's also the most capital-starved book in the experiment: $13.54 of cash against $9,543 deployed across 59 open positions. It has been effectively unable to take a full-size signal for weeks — its worst loss (−$504 on an Apple market-cap bet) and an 18.3% drawdown came from exactly the resolution-holds it can't shed.

Strengths: the mirror-exit mechanism is validated — it's the only algo where "follow the exit too" is testable, and that half of the ledger is green.

Weaknesses: everything it can't mirror out of. Sources that hold to resolution transfer their bag-holding to us; full deployment plus no TP/SL means the losers sit; return −5.9%.

VerdictHalf a good algo. Add a time-stop or TP/SL fallback for positions the source never exits, and free up the bankroll — the mirror half deserves better capital.

Inverse Losers: the relapse

Three weeks ago we wrote the post-mortem: fading habitual losers concentrated 86% of its book on one Fed meeting, the losers bet the favorite, the favorite hit, first place became last. We prescribed a per-event concentration cap and a don't-auto-fade-favorites rule.

Since then it has settled exactly two positions (+$33 net — a Minecraft-challenge win, an Iranian-blockade loss), still sits at 3-for-11 lifetime with a 0.34 profit factor and −7.5%… and here is its current open book: seven of fourteen positions, 59% of deployed cost, are September FOMC markets. The concentration cap never shipped. The fish are still loudest at the Fed, the filter still screens out the sports markets where losing bettors actually live, and so the algo has faithfully rebuilt the exact book that blew it up in July, one event later.

Strengths: intellectually honest — it's the only algo testing a fade, and eleven settles genuinely isn't a verdict on the hypothesis. Signal scarcity, not signal quality, is the binding constraint.

Weaknesses: everything the July post-mortem said, still true, plus the new one: we didn't implement our own recommendations. An algo that re-concentrates into the next scheduled macro event after a concentration post-mortem isn't unlucky; it's unmaintained.

VerdictShip the event cap and the favorite-fade price condition before September 16, or retire it. Running the same experiment twice and calling it new data is the one thing we said we wouldn't do.

The thing all six classified books share

Every algo except Top 10 Mirror drinks from the same classified-wallet stream, and it shows. The Strait of Hormuz market family appears in six of seven open books. The same two source wallets are the top signal source for most of them. On July 29 the four longest-running algos hit their peak at the exact same snapshot — 20:28 UTC, the July FOMC resolution — and all four hit their trough at the exact same snapshot three days later. These aren't seven independent experiments; they're closer to five correlated expressions of one whale-flow bet, plus a control, plus a sports book.

Which makes the current setup worth staring at:

What 33 days actually taught us

Exits made the money, entries just picked the fights

Every profitable mechanism this month was an exit rule: Kitchen Sink's take-profits (+$817) and 25-for-27 latch (+$507), Crowd Surge's take-profits (+$1,177), Sharp Follow's mirror exits (+$751), Top 10 Mirror's daily resolution churn. Every major bleed was a hold-to-resolution: Sharp Follow's un-mirrored holds (−$1,194), Insider Echo's WTI ride to zero, Inverse Losers' entire lifetime. The signal research keeps getting the attention; the exit policy keeps earning the returns.

The control is the most valuable algo we run

Everyman's realized PF of 0.71 versus Kitchen Sink's 1.87 on the same stream is the whole case for the classification layer, made in one comparison. No individual return number on the board is as informative. Every future experiment gets a control from day one.

Correlation is the unmanaged risk, and it's already reloaded

Six books, $7,077, one September Fed meeting — after July's FOMC hit every classified algo at the same timestamp. Per-event exposure caps were recommended in the July 30 post-mortem and never implemented. That's now the highest-priority engineering item, above any new strategy.

Paper leads lie; realized PnL doesn't

Ranked by scoreboard equity, the experiment looks like a gentle success. Ranked by realized PnL, it's one working algo, three break-evens, and three donors. We keep re-learning this and the scoreboard keeps re-teaching it: 99% of banked profit lives in the one book that settles 16 positions a day.

Month two, then: ship the event cap, give Insider Echo and Sharp Follow an exit stack, decide Inverse Losers' fate before the September 16 FOMC, and let Top 10 Mirror keep printing until it teaches us its own next lesson — which, on current volatility, it will.
