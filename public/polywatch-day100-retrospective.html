100 days of paper trading: one algo tripled, and two turned out to be the same algo

On day 100, Top 10 Mirror is at $31,435 from $10,000. Crowd Surge and Kitchen Sink finished within $15 of each other at roughly +17.8%. That isn't a coincidence: they take 94% of the same trades, so we're retiring one. Meanwhile, Top 10 Mirror has been running in shadow against a real exchange for almost four weeks. It matches paper trade-for-trade, but copies only about two-thirds of paper's trades, and we've set the bar it has to clear before it trades real money.

Crowd Surge and Kitchen Sink are one algorithm with two exits

On paper they're different ideas. Crowd Surge enters when two or more tracked wallets buy the same outcome within 24 hours. Kitchen Sink adds up a conviction score: +3 for an insider, +2 for smart money, +1 for a decent whale, +1 for a $25K+ trade, and +1 for each extra wallet. It enters at 4. One counts wallets; the other weighs them.

In practice the score crosses 4 almost exactly when a second tracked wallet joins in. Two smart-money wallets already score 5. So both algorithms fire on the same events, usually within minutes of each other:

The 16 markets only Crowd Surge entered and the 9 only Kitchen Sink entered add up to about 6% of either book. Everything else is the same trade, entered at the same moment.

The real difference is the exit. Crowd Surge takes profit at +50%. Kitchen Sink waits for +60%, and once a position is marked above 90¢ it rides to resolution. As a result Crowd Surge cashes out early twice as often. Kitchen Sink makes roughly the same money by holding more of its winners to the end, and the stop-losses cost both the same.

Daily returns are correlated at 0.76. The two curves have drifted up to $709 apart, but they always came back together. So for 100 days we've been running an A/B test of two exit policies on identical entries without meaning to, and it's a tie.

One caveat applies to both: only about half the gain is banked. Realized profit is +$846 and +$682. The rest is unrealized, spread over the 65 to 67 positions each algo still has open.

VerdictThey're redundant, and merging them doesn't produce anything new. Merge the entry rules and you get the same entries; pick either exit and history says you get the same result. We're retiring Kitchen Sink and keeping Crowd Surge. Crowd Surge's rule is the simpler one, explains itself in a sentence, and has settled more positions. Kitchen Sink stops opening new positions today, and its open book runs to resolution the way Inverse Losers' did. Its seat is free for an idea that doesn't already exist on the board.

Shadow live trading: matching every trade it takes, but not taking enough

Since September 28, Top 10 Mirror has had a shadow: a $1,000 book that turns each of its signals into an order against Polymarket US. That's the regulated US exchange and the only venue we'll put real money on. The shadow uses real US order books, real prices and real fees, and sends nothing. It answers one question: if we flipped the switch, would the live book do what the paper book does?

For the trades it takes, the answer is yes. Since October 6 the shadow only enters when paper enters. Of the 98 positions both books have settled, all 98 came out the same way, and the shadow's average return per position is 1.3 points below paper's. That gap is about the exchange fees, which average 3.1% of cost.

The problem is how much it takes. Paper copies wallets on Polymarket's international exchange; the shadow has to place every one of those trades on a separate exchange with its own catalog and its own market IDs. Measured by paper's cost, the shadow copied 53% of paper's trading in the first week of October, 62% the next week, and 67% the week after that.

What's been hard

1 · The two exchanges don't share a key

Every trade has to be translated from one exchange's market to the other's. Team codes differ (epl-che-bou vs epl-cfc-bor), league prefixes differ, and tennis uses different player codes entirely (medvede-paul vs danmed-tompau). For most US leagues and esports a shared game ID bridges the gap. For tennis and the European cups, the US side doesn't carry one.

2 · Some markets simply aren't there

NATO-versus-Russia contracts, small tennis challengers and first-half spreads all exist on the international exchange and not on the US one. That was almost 10% of paper's cost in the window above, and no amount of engineering recovers it.

3 · The market is sometimes closed when the signal arrives

Some NFL, MLB and NBA markets map correctly but show as not open at the moment a source wallet buys. We don't yet know if that's a real pre-game window on the US side or a timing problem on ours.

4 · The first two weeks were plumbing

Day one produced zero fills: every signal failed to map. Since then we've fixed night games dated a day apart on the two exchanges, a spread ladder quoted from one team's side, rate-limit errors during bursts of signals on the same game, and the esports and soccer slug mismatches. Each fix moved coverage up a few points.

One number made us more patient about all this. Over the last three weeks, the paper trades the shadow missed netted −$45: 45 winners, 35 losers. Missing them hasn't cost the shadow any edge so far; it has only made the shadow a noisier copy of paper. That changed what we're measuring. Coverage isn't a profit test. It's a fidelity test.

The decision: an 80% fidelity bar

We considered making paper trade US-listed markets only. That would make the two books match by definition, and it would prove nothing. Paper stays on the international exchange as the benchmark. Instead we've defined what the shadow is graded on:

The go-live barFidelity = the share of paper's cost the shadow copied, out of the paper cost it could have copied. Markets Polymarket US doesn't list are excluded; everything US does list counts, whatever the reason we missed it. A market we can't confirm either way also counts as a miss. Real money goes in after two consecutive weeks at 80% or better.

On that definition, October 12–23 comes out at about 74%. With unlisted markets set aside, the most the shadow could reach is around 90%. Three pieces of work close the gap:

Match by start time and names

When the slug and the game ID both fail, look up the US events that start within an hour of the international one and match on the players or teams. Tennis and the European cups were 14 points of paper's cost on their own, enough to clear 80% in a typical week.

Find out why markets read as closed

Another 5.5 points belongs to games that already map. If it's a pre-open window, we can queue the entry; if it's a brief status flip, we can retry.

Report fidelity every week

The shadow's daily report will carry the week's fidelity and a count of consecutive weeks at 80% or better, plus the list of markets excluded as unlisted, so a translation miss can't hide among them.

Politics and macro markets stay paper-only for now. Only about a third of them have a US version, and they're worth about two points. We'll come back to them once the sports book is clearing the bar.

So day 100 looks like this. The algorithm worth trading has tripled on paper. Its shadow copy gets every trade it takes right. Real money waits on two weeks at 80% fidelity.
