Top 10 Mirror is up 130%. Here's every rule change that got it there.

Forty-five days after we wiped Top 10 Mirror and rebuilt it, the algorithm has turned $10,000 into $22,959 on 1,682 settled positions. This is the post-mortem we've been waiting to write: which decisions produced the gains, which wallets actually delivered them, and — because the numbers insist on it — how concentrated the whole thing is.

The first thing we ever published about this project was a post-mortem titled "It Lost Money — Fast." Top 10 Mirror v1 copied the ten wallets at the top of Polymarket's all-time profit leaderboard, discovered that eight of them were dormant and two were arbitrage bots, and gave back 8% of its bankroll in twelve days. On July 28 we wiped the book, reset the cash to $10,000, and restarted with a different theory of who deserves a seat. Every post since has been about something going wrong. This one is about the thing that went right, written with the same rigor we'd apply if it hadn't.

What the algorithm actually does

Every six hours, Top 10 Mirror pulls Polymarket's profit leaderboards — all-time, 30-day and 7-day — and ranks the union of them by lifetime profit. A wallet earns one of ten seats if it clears a stack of gates: it has traded in the last 60 days, it isn't a bot, its history is at least 90 days old, it hasn't lost a quarter of its lifetime record in the past month, and it has qualified on three consecutive refreshes. Seated wallets are polled every minute. When one buys, we buy the same outcome, sized to how big their bet was relative to their scale, capped at 10% of equity per market and 15% per source. When they sell, we sell. Otherwise we hold to resolution. That's it — no take-profit, no stop-loss, no opinion about the match.

The current roster holds nine wallets, with four more waiting out the hysteresis window. Since July 28 the mirror has copied 95 different wallets across 3,526 buys and $197,594 of notional — about twenty turns of the bankroll in six and a half weeks.

The five decisions

The gains didn't come from one insight. They came from a sequence of rule changes, each one made in response to a specific failure, and three of them were reversals of things we'd shipped earlier. Here they are in the order they mattered.

Decision 1 · July 27Rank by lifetime profit, not the hot streak

v1 seated whoever topped the all-time board and got arb bots and dormant whales. The obvious fix — rank by 30-day profit — would have seated whoever was on a heater, and heaters end. v3 split the difference: candidates come from every window, but the ranking key is lifetime PnL. A losing month doesn't eject a proven winner; only a catastrophic one does. The very first roster this rule produced included a wallet named beachboy4, seated at 01:59 UTC on July 28. It is the only seat that has never turned over, and it has since made $5,770 — 45% of everything the algorithm has realized.

Decision 2 · July 27, undone August 17, restored September 2Keep the bots out — and put the gate back when we took it out

The v3 bot gate was simple: a wallet trading more than five times an hour, or holding both sides of more than one market, is a market-maker and doesn't get a seat. On August 17 we replaced it with something smarter — a matched-notional arbitrage test that let directional wallets trade as fast as they liked — because the frequency ceiling was excluding a high-clip accumulator we wanted to copy. Here is what the two regimes did:

The arb test detected arbitrage and said nothing about volume. Sports scalpers running 10 to 55 trades an hour passed cleanly, capital deployed went from $2,036 a day to $10,516 a day, and one anonymous wallet alone generated 443 of our settled positions and lost $2,556 on them. Every paper fill pays a penny of modeled slippage, so at five times the turnover the structural drag went from 0.4% of bankroll per day to 1.7%. The five-per-hour ceiling had been doing a job nobody named: it was the turnover limiter. We put it back on September 2. The positions opened since are 146-for-230 and have returned 22.6% on cost.

The diagnosis that kept us goingOn August 25, with equity at $8,793 and the book down 12%, we ran the numbers gross of slippage: the mirror signal was +$1,013. Slippage paid was $2,085. The sign of the P&L had been flipped by turnover, not by the quality of the copies. That's the finding that made "fix the turnover" the answer instead of "retire the algo." Total slippage paid to date is $3,752, so the same book gross of that drag is up roughly $16,500.

Decision 3 · July 16Copy the exit, not just the entry

The mirror keeps a separate lot for every source wallet in every market. When a source sells, we sell that lot, at the market's price at that moment, and nothing else. This is older than v3 — it dates from the original engine build — but it is the reason the algorithm can hold to resolution without a stop-loss. 244 positions have closed on a mirrored sell, 137 of them winners, for +$2,355. The other 1,438 rode to resolution: 821 winners, +$10,390. The sources' own risk management is doing the work a stop-loss would do, and doing it with information we don't have.

Decision 4 · July 15, tightened August 17 and 23Size to conviction and to equity

A copy is sized as a fraction of current equity — 5% at full scale — multiplied by how large the source's bet was relative to a $100,000 reference. A wallet nibbling $500 gets us a $25 position; a wallet swinging $50,000 gets us $2,500. Two things follow. First, the copies are biggest exactly where the source was surest. Second, because the fraction is of equity rather than starting cash, the same rule that placed $500 bets in July was placing $1,100 bets by September. The run from $14,000 to $23,000 in the second week of September is partly the sources winning and partly compounding doing what compounding does.

The caps arrived later, each after a bruise: 10% of equity per market from day one, a halved cap for wallets seated less than a week (August 17), and a 15% aggregate cap per source wallet (August 23). None of them make money. All of them are why a bad weekend is a drawdown and not a wipeout.

Decision 5 · August 23–24Cut the tail after the worst weekend

The Djdjdjekekek weekend cost $1,958 and taught us that lifetime ranking is blind to young wallets: for a 48-day-old account, the lifetime record is the streak. Three rules shipped within 48 hours. A wallet's history must be at least 90 days old to be seated. No source can hold more than 15% of equity in open positions. And a newcomer has to qualify on three consecutive refreshes — twelve hours minimum — before it gets a seat, while drops remain immediate. Slow to trust, fast to cut. The positions opened after those rules went live are 408-for-671 and have made $12,831; the positions opened in the seven days before them lost $4,137.

What we're not going to pretend

The five wallets above sum to $12,286 — 96% of realized profit. The other ninety sources, across 1,540 settled positions and $139,445 deployed, netted +$459. That's a return of 0.3% on the bulk of the book. Read one way, the algorithm's job is to find the beachboy4s and stay out of the way while the long tail washes; the gates and caps exist so the tail can't take the book down while it does. Read the other way, five wallets is not a sample, and one of them was seated for a day.

The timing is lumpy, too. The week of September 7 produced $8,626 of realized profit on 106 settles — two-thirds of the all-time figure in five days, with a 25¢ tennis upset at the center of it. Strip the Shelton position out and the mirror is still up 101%, which is still the best thing we've built; but the difference between a 100% algorithm and a 130% one, on this book, is one match.

And it is, so far, a sports algorithm. Of 1,682 settles, 1,500 were sports and made $12,830. The 182 non-sports positions — Fed decisions, geopolitics, crypto, Minecraft challenges — netted −$84. The leaderboard's genuine bettors are sports bettors, and the copies inherit that. Whether the same seat rules would find edge in election season is a question the data can't answer yet.

What we do believeForty-five days, 1,682 settles, four rule eras, and a full round trip through a 37% drawdown. Three of the four rule eras were profitable on cost; the one that lost money ran the loosened gate before any cap on the tail existed. That's the claim the evidence actually supports: not that the mirror has a 130% edge, but that the seat rules reliably put the book on genuine bettors, and genuine bettors at the top of Polymarket's lifetime board have been worth copying. The size of the payoff has been decided by a handful of them.

What we're taking from this

1 · The signal was fine; the plumbing was the P&L

Three of the five decisions were about turnover, sizing and caps, not about who to copy. The one time we loosened the plumbing to improve the signal, we lost 6% in a week. Gross of slippage, the copies have been positive in every era.

2 · Rank on the long record, gate on the short one

Lifetime profit picks the wallets; liveness, seasoning and the 30-day loss cap decide whether the record is still live and still theirs. Splitting the ranking key from the gates is what let a July seat still be earning in September.

3 · Reversals are decisions too

The gate revert and the liveness widening were both us undoing our own work with a number attached. The willingness to ship "we were wrong on August 17" on September 2 is worth more than either rule.

4 · Diagnose at the bottom, not the top

The most useful analysis in this project happened at $8,793, and it said the losses were mechanical. Had we judged the algorithm on its equity curve that morning, it would be retired, and this post would be another post-mortem.

5 · Report the concentration with the return

Ninety-six percent from five wallets and twenty-two percent from one match are not footnotes; they're the shape of the result. The next checkpoint for this algorithm is whether the sixth, seventh and eighth wallets show up.

Top 10 Mirror is still running the September 2 rules unchanged, with nine seats filled and four wallets in the hysteresis queue. The next scheduled look is day 80, alongside Insider Echo's probation review. Between now and then the roster gets to prove that the beachboy4 seat was the rule working, and not the rule getting lucky.
