We copied Polymarket's ten most profitable traders. It lost money — fast.

A post-mortem of Top 10 Mirror v2, the three structural reasons the obvious idea fails, and what v3 does differently.

Polywatch runs seven paper-trading algorithms against live Polymarket data, each seeded with $10,000, to test which whale-copying strategies actually work. Top 10 Mirror had the most intuitive thesis of the lot: copy every trade made by the ten most profitable wallets on the all-time leaderboard, and exit when they exit. If anyone on Polymarket knows something, surely it's the people who've won the most money.

Eleven days after launch it had turned $10,000 into $9,086 across 1,169 resolved positions. That's not unlucky — that's a machine doing exactly what it was told, where what it was told turns out to be structurally broken. We audited the whole thing. Here's what we found.

The top of the leaderboard isn't who you think it is

The all-time profit leaderboard selects for total dollars extracted, and the most effective way to extract dollars from a prediction market is not prescience — it's high-frequency arbitrage. Of our ten roster wallets, exactly two ever produced a fill, and both are algorithmic sports-arb operations. One of them executed 500 trades in 12.7 minutes when we sampled it — roughly 40 trades per minute, nearly all of them buys, across tennis matches and MLB games. The other eight seats were mostly dormant legacy whales from the 2024 election cycle whose occasional trades were too small to copy.

So "mirror the ten best traders" was, in practice, "mirror two arbitrage bots." And an arb bot's profit comes from execution — maker fills, speed, and hedged books — which is precisely the part a copier cannot have.

60-second explainer — prediction-market arbitrage

Every Polymarket market has YES shares and NO shares, and at resolution exactly one of them pays $1. That means owning one YES and one NO is worth exactly $1, guaranteed, no matter what happens in the world.

So whenever the two prices sum to less than a dollar, there's free money. If YES trades at 48¢ and NO at 49¢, buy both for 97¢ and you're holding a guaranteed dollar — a risk-free 3¢. No opinion about the match required. The bots at the top of the leaderboard scan thousands of markets for these gaps and take them within seconds, over and over, at size. Three cents doesn't sound like much until you do it forty times a minute during every tennis match on Earth.

Which is why copying them is hopeless: the profit isn't in what they buy, it's in the pair-price and the speed. Copy one leg without the other and you've made a naked bet the bot never made. Copy both legs a minute later at a cent worse per side, and the 97¢ pair costs you 99¢ — the entire margin just went to whoever sold it to you.

From there, three specific failures follow.

Failure #1 — the exit half never ran. Not once.

The algo's rule was "exit when they exit." Across 1,169 resolved positions, the number of mirror exits was zero. These wallets never sell: they buy YES, buy NO, and either merge the pair for a guaranteed dollar or hold to resolution and redeem. Our watcher only reacted to SELL trades — which never came. Half the strategy was structurally dead code, and every copied position rode to resolution whether the source wallet was still exposed or not.

Failure #2 — slippage was bigger than the edge

The paper engine charges a 1¢ slippage haircut on every entry, a deliberately conservative stand-in for crossing the spread. For copying a directional bettor whose edge is several cents of mispriced probability, that's a fair tax. For copying an arbitrageur whose entire edge is one or two cents of execution, it's fatal:

Read that again: the bots' picks, even sampled and delayed, were gross profitable. The mirror converted a thin winning strategy into a losing one purely by paying one cent more per share than the wallets it copied.

Failure #3 — copying a hedge without the hedge

This was the biggest dollar loser, and the subtlest. Arb bots buy both outcomes of the same market when the prices sum below $1. Copy both legs faithfully and you lock in a small guaranteed loss (you paid slippage twice on a pair that pays exactly $1). But it gets worse: our position sizing — dust floors, per-market exposure caps, and a 50-entry polling window — clipped or dropped legs independently. The mirror routinely ended up holding one unhedged side of somebody else's hedge, at a worse price.

A warning about the data, for anyone who tries this

Two API landmines we hit that anyone analyzing Polymarket wallets should know. First, the leaderboard API offers exactly four windows — 1d, 7d, 30d, all-time — and its board is hard-capped at 50 rows. There is no 12-month view. Second, you cannot reconstruct a wallet's PnL by summing its closed positions: positions held to worthless expiry never get a closed-position row. One roster wallet's closed positions summed to $11.7M of "profit" against an official lifetime figure of $4.3M — the missing $7.4M is losses that simply have no row. Any ranking built on that sum is a ranking of gross winners, i.e. of volume.

What v3 does instead

The machinery was fine; the roster was wrong. v3 keeps the mirror and rebuilds selection around one question — is this a genuine bettor whose lifetime record proves an edge? — with four rules:

Bot gate

Reject wallets trading over 5 times per hour, or buying both sides of more than one market. In our data the two populations don't overlap: bots run 50–1,100 trades/hour, humans run 0.1–4. There's an empty order of magnitude between them.

Rank by lifetime PnL

Official leaderboard numbers only, pooled from the all-time, 30-day, and 7-day boards. One hot month is not evidence of skill — the 30-day board's #9 turned out to be lifetime-negative.

Liveness floor

At least 20 trades in the last 30 days. The all-time board is full of retired 2024-election whales who would fill exactly zero positions.

Scale-adjusted drawdown cap

Eject a wallet only if one month erases more than 25% of its lifetime profit. A whale staking seven figures per market can lose $500K in a routine week — a fixed dollar cap would eject skill and keep tilt.

The roster re-vets itself every six hours; names churn in and out as the leaderboard moves, but the rules don't.

What the experiment now tests

v3 relaunched today with a fresh $10,000 and a clean start. The honest framing: v2 never tested whether top traders are worth copying — it tested whether you can copy market makers, and the answer is obviously no. v3 finally asks the real question: does a proven directional bettor's edge survive a 1¢ copying tax? If their mispricing edge is real, it's several cents wide and should clear the haircut. If the mirror still bleeds, that's evidence the edge lives in timing and price we can't replicate — which would be a genuinely interesting negative result.

Either way, the leaderboard re-vets itself every six hours, every rule is a number in a config file, and the scoreboard is public. We'll report back.
