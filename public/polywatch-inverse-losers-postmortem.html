How Inverse Losers went from first place to last in one Fed announcement — and why its six-day lead was never real to begin with.

Polywatch runs seven paper-trading algorithms against live Polymarket data, each seeded with $10,000. Inverse Losers is the contrarian of the group: it watches wallets that have lost at least $5,000 lifetime across ten or more resolved bets, and whenever one of them makes a big buy, it buys the opposite outcome. If someone has reliably torched money for years, surely the other side of their trade is a good place to stand.

For six days it was the best-performing algo on the board, peaking at $11,428 on July 28. On the evening of July 29 the Federal Reserve announced its July decision, and by the next snapshot Inverse Losers was at $9,255 — dead last. First to worst in one press release. Here's the autopsy.

The whole book was one bet

Inverse Losers has made exactly ten entries in its life. Eight of them were the same trade: the July 2026 FOMC meeting. Its source wallets — nine losing wallets with lifetime PnL ranging from −$24K to −$7.2M, roughly $17 million of combined losses — kept buying "the Fed holds rates" at 72–93¢. So the algo did its job and bought the other side: "rates change" tokens at 7–28¢, seven separate times, plus one hedged leg. Of the $1,332 the algo has ever deployed, 86% was riding on a single announcement.

That concentration wasn't a fluke; it was structural. Fish signals are rare — big buys from qualifying losers arrive at less than one per day — and the pipeline filters out sports markets, which is where losing bettors actually live. What leaks through the filter is macro. And in late July, the only macro market that mattered was the Fed. Six of the ten entries even arrived in a single batch, when the v3 relaunch backfilled the previous days of fish signals — a window in which the loudest fish trade was, of course, the FOMC.

The lead was paper

Watch the chart again. The climb to first place and the fall to last place are the same positions. Mid-week, markets started flirting with a rate cut, and the "rates change" tokens the algo had bought for an average of about 14¢ marked up past 30¢. At the July 28 peak, positions bought for $1,144 carried a mark-to-market value of $2,368 — every dollar of the lead was unrealized gains on unresolved longshots. Then the Fed held. The change-side tokens went to zero, the one hedged leg paid $200, and the scoreboard position evaporated along with the marks.

60-second explainer — why a longshot book lies to the scoreboard

A prediction-market token resolves to exactly $1 or exactly $0. Until then, its price is a probability, and mark-to-market equity is just that probability times your shares. A book full of 14¢ longshots can double on paper any time the market's mood shifts a few points — no trade has to go right, nothing has to resolve, the poll just moved.

That means an equity leaderboard over unresolved binary positions rewards volatility exposure, not skill, right up until resolution day. Inverse Losers "led" for six days because it held the twitchiest tokens on the board during a week of rate-cut speculation. The scoreboard was reporting a poll. Resolution reported the result: those $2,368 of marks were worth $200.

The fish bought the favorite. That matters.

Here's the part that stings beyond one bad Fed call. The premise of Inverse Losers is that habitual losers carry negative information — stand on the other side and their anti-edge becomes your edge. But that logic quietly assumes the loser is doing something contrarian. This week they weren't. The fish bought "Fed holds" at 72–93¢ — the consensus, the favorite, the thing that then happened.

When a loser bets the favorite, fading them forces you into the longshot, and prediction markets share a well-documented pathology with racetracks: favorite–longshot bias. Longshots are systematically overpriced, because someone is always willing to pay a little too much for a lottery ticket. So this particular shape of fade — buying 14¢ tokens because a bad bettor bought the 86¢ side — starts from behind before you even ask whether the fish knows anything. People who have lost $7 million on Polymarket mostly didn't lose it by betting favorites; they lost it on sizing, timing, and chasing. Their favorite bets can be perfectly fine, and this week, all of them were.

The gate selects for bad lifetime PnL. It does not select for being wrong on this market. Those are different things, and the difference just cost the algo its lead.

What changes

The honest caveat first: ten positions is not a verdict on fading losers. One correlated event went against the book; that's variance. What the week did expose are three construction flaws that would hurt no matter how the Fed voted:

Concentration cap

86% of lifetime deployment on one event means the algo's fate was a coin flip, not a strategy. A per-event exposure cap — the Fed's eight July markets were all one announcement — keeps a single macro date from becoming the whole book.

Don't auto-fade favorites

Fading a fish who buys at 85¢ means buying a 15¢ longshot and eating favorite–longshot bias. The signal may only be worth acting on when the fish is off-consensus — a price condition on the fade, not just a PnL condition on the fish.

Feed the algo or shrink its claims

Under one qualifying signal per day, every conclusion drowns in noise. The fish population lives in sports markets, which the pipeline currently filters out. Either let the fade see sports, or accept that this experiment needs months, not weeks.

And one lesson for us rather than the algo: we watched the scoreboard all week and told ourselves a story about our clever contrarian. The equity rank of a longshot book is a mood ring. Next time an algo "leads" on unrealized marks over unresolved binaries, the correct reaction is not pride — it's checking what single event is propping up the number.
